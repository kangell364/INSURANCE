### A policy covers every cause of loss except those it excludes. What basis is that?

- [ ] Named peril
- [x] Open peril
- [ ] Broad form
- [ ] Basic form

**Open peril** — also called **special form**, and in older usage *all risk*.
Coverage is everything the policy does not take away, so the **exclusions**
decide how broad it really is.

Under **named peril**, the policy lists what it covers and anything unlisted
is uncovered. **Basic** and **broad** are named-peril lists of different
lengths, which is why they are wrong here rather than merely narrower.

### Under a named peril policy, who must show what caused the loss?

- [x] The insured, who must show the cause is a listed peril
- [ ] The insurer, which must show the cause is excluded
- [ ] Neither; the adjuster decides
- [ ] Whichever party requests appraisal

**Named peril puts the burden on the insured.** The list is the coverage, so a
claim means showing the loss came from something on it.

**Open peril flips it.** Coverage is presumed, and the insurer must bring the
loss within an exclusion to deny it. That shift in who has to prove what is
the practical difference between the two bases.

### A ten-year-old roof is destroyed. It would cost $20,000 to replace new, and it was half way through its life. What does actual cash value pay?

- [ ] $20,000
- [x] $10,000 — replacement cost less depreciation
- [ ] $20,000 less the deductible only
- [ ] Whatever the roof would have sold for

**Actual cash value is replacement cost less depreciation.** Half the life
used, half the value gone: $20,000 less $10,000.

**Replacement cost** would pay the full $20,000, ignoring age. Which basis a
form uses is worth a great deal of money and is a recurring fact in Module 1.

Market value is a different measure again — what the property would sell for,
which for a roof considered alone is not a meaningful figure.
