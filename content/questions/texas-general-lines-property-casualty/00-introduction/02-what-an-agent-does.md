### An insurance agent legally represents whom?

- [ ] The applicant, who pays the premium
- [x] The insurer, as its agent under the law of agency
- [ ] Both equally
- [ ] The Texas Department of Insurance

**The insurer is the principal; the agent acts for it.** Within the authority
granted, the agent's acts are the company's acts — which is why the agent's
knowledge is imputed to the company and why a misstatement about coverage can
bind it.

Note the exception the exam likes: a **broker** represents the **buyer**, not
the insurer. That is what makes a broker a broker.

### An applicant mentions in passing that they heat with a wood stove. The producer does not record it. Whose view of the risk is now wrong?

- [ ] Nobody's — an unrecorded remark has no effect
- [x] The insurer's, because the application is all the underwriter sees
- [ ] The applicant's, for failing to insist it be written down
- [ ] The adjuster's, but only once a claim is made

**The company's** — and that is what field underwriting means. The producer is
the only person in the transaction who stands in front of the risk. The
underwriter sees the application and nothing else, so what the producer asks,
observes and writes down *becomes* the company's view of the risk.

That is why an omission on an application is not a clerical matter. An
application that leaves out the wood stove has misdescribed the risk the
company agreed to take.

### A licensed agent has no appointment from a particular insurer. What may they do for that insurer?

- [ ] Anything, because the licence is what the state issues
- [x] Nothing — a licence permits transacting insurance, but an appointment is the company's authority to act for it
- [ ] Only service existing policies
- [ ] Only sell personal lines

**Two separate permissions.** The **licence** is the state's, permitting you to
transact insurance at all. The **appointment** is the company's, authorising
you to act for that company.

You need both, and the exam tests the distinction directly.
