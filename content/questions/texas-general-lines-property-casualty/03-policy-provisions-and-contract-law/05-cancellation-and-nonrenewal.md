### An insurer cancels a policy at the 200-day mark of an annual term. Pro rata or short rate?

- [x] Pro rata — the insurer cancelled
- [ ] Short rate, because more than half the term has run
- [ ] Flat, because the policy was in force
- [ ] Short rate, because the return premium is calculated the same either way

**Insurer cancels → pro rata. Insured cancels → short rate.** The party who
caused the early ending bears the cost of it, and the insured who did not
choose to end the policy is not penalised.

### A policy has been in force four years. The insurer wants to cancel because the underwriter has re-evaluated the class of business. Permitted?

- [ ] Yes, an insurer may cancel for any lawful reason with proper notice
- [x] No — after the underwriting window the insurer is limited to permitted grounds
- [ ] Yes, provided the insurer offers 60 days' notice
- [ ] Only if the insured has had a claim in the period

**During the first 60 days** an insurer may generally cancel for any lawful
reason. After that, the list narrows to non-payment, material misrepresentation
or fraud, a substantial increase in hazard, loss of reinsurance, and violation
of the policy's terms or of insurance law. Second thoughts is not on it.

### An insured cancels an $1,800 annual policy after six months. Will the return premium be more or less than $900?

- [ ] More, because the insurer retains no expenses
- [x] Less — short rate deducts a penalty from the pro rata return
- [ ] Exactly $900
- [ ] Nothing is returned once half the term has run

Short rate charges the insured for **time in force plus a penalty** covering
the insurer's unrecovered expenses. The return is always less than the pro rata
figure.

### The insurer decides not to renew but sends no notice. What is the usual consequence?

- [x] The policy renews for another term
- [ ] The policy expires anyway; notice is a courtesy
- [ ] The policy continues month to month until notice is given
- [ ] The insured may demand a new policy from the state pool

**No notice generally means the policy renews.** That consequence is the
practical reason insurers treat the non-renewal deadline — commonly 30 days
before expiry — as seriously as they do.

### A policy is cancelled the day before its effective date. What is returned?

- [x] The entire premium — a flat cancellation
- [ ] The pro rata unearned premium
- [ ] The short rate return
- [ ] Nothing, because the policy was issued

**Flat cancellation** applies where the policy is cancelled before it takes
effect or before any premium is earned. No premium was earned, so all of it
goes back.

### Which notice period most commonly applies to cancellation for non-payment of premium?

- [x] 10 days
- [ ] 30 days
- [ ] 45 days
- [ ] 60 days

The general pattern is **10 days for non-payment**, **30 days for any other
reason**, and **30 days before expiry for non-renewal**. Texas sets its own
figures in Chapter 551 of the Insurance Code, and a Texas exam question is
testing those.

### What is the difference between rescission and cancellation?

- [x] Rescission unwinds the policy from inception; cancellation ends it going forward
- [ ] Rescission requires 30 days' notice; cancellation requires 10
- [ ] Rescission applies only to non-payment
- [ ] There is no difference; the terms are interchangeable

**Rescission** treats the policy as though it never existed — usually for
material misrepresentation — and returns the premium. **Cancellation** ends
coverage prospectively. **Declination** is a refusal to issue at all, so no
policy ever existed.

### Where a policy names four insureds, who may cancel it?

- [x] The first named insured
- [ ] Any one of the four
- [ ] All four acting together
- [ ] Whichever insured paid the most recent premium

Cancellation is one of the **first named insured's** rights, alongside
receiving notices, requesting changes and receiving return premium.

### An insurer mails a cancellation notice to the address on the declarations. The insured says it never arrived. Is the notice effective?

- [x] Yes — proof of mailing is generally proof of notice
- [ ] No, the insurer must prove actual receipt
- [ ] No, unless the notice was sent by certified mail with a return receipt
- [ ] Only if the insured later admits having received it

Notice must be **in writing, to the first named insured, at the address shown
on the declarations**. Mailing is generally sufficient; the insurer does not
have to show the insured read it.

### At renewal the insurer proposes a substantially higher deductible and a new exclusion. What does this usually trigger?

- [x] The same advance notice requirement as a non-renewal
- [ ] Nothing; renewal terms are the insurer's to set freely
- [ ] An immediate right of rescission for the insured
- [ ] A 10-day cancellation notice

A material change in terms at renewal is treated like a non-renewal because the
insured **needs time to shop**. Many states also require the insurer to state
its reason on request.
