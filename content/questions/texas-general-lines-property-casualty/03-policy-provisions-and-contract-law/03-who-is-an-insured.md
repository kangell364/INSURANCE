### A warehouse negligently destroys goods it is storing. The owner's property policy pays the owner. What does the "no benefit to bailee" clause achieve?

- [x] It preserves the insurer's subrogation against the warehouse
- [ ] It lets the insurer refuse the owner's claim
- [ ] It requires the warehouse to reimburse the owner directly
- [ ] It extends the owner's coverage to the warehouse's liability

**The owner is paid as normal — the clause is not aimed at them.** It stops the
**bailee** taking any benefit from the owner's policy.

**Without it the insurer would have nobody to pursue.** A negligent warehouse
could shelter behind the very policy that paid for its negligence, and the
insurer's subrogation right would be worth nothing.

**This is why bailees buy their own coverage.** A dry cleaner or warehouse
cannot rely on its customers' policies, so it buys **bailee's customers**
coverage on its own account.

Compare the **standard mortgage clause**, which does the opposite for a
different third party: it gives the mortgagee rights **beyond** the insured's.
One provision lets a third party in, the other keeps one out, and both exist
to put the loss where it belongs.

### A commercial policy names three entities. To whom must the insurer send notice of cancellation?

- [x] The first named insured, which is effective against all of them
- [ ] All three, separately
- [ ] Whichever entity paid the premium
- [ ] The entity whose operations gave rise to the cancellation

The **first named insured** receives notices on behalf of every insured on the
policy. The same position carries the premium obligation, the right to cancel,
the right to request changes and responsibility for any audit.

### An insured deliberately burns down a mortgaged building. The policy carries a standard mortgage clause. Is the mortgagee paid?

- [x] Yes, provided the mortgagee took no part in the arson
- [ ] No — arson voids the policy for everyone named on it
- [ ] Only up to the actual cash value of the building
- [ ] Only if the mortgagee submitted its own proof of loss first

A **standard** (or union) mortgage clause protects the lender from the
*insured's* acts. The insurer pays, and then acquires the mortgagee's rights
against the insured to the extent of the payment.

### Same arson, but the policy carries an open loss payable clause. Is the lender paid?

- [ ] Yes, an open clause and a standard clause protect the lender equally
- [x] No — an open clause gives the lender no rights greater than the insured's
- [ ] Yes, but only the unpaid loan balance
- [ ] Only if the lender had no knowledge of the arson

An **open** clause merely directs payment. It rides on the insured's coverage,
so when the insured's coverage is void the lender collects nothing. "Standard
protects the mortgagee from the insured's acts; open does not."

### A subcontractor's certificate of insurance names the general contractor as certificate holder. A claim arises from the sub's work. Is the general contractor an insured?

- [ ] Yes, the certificate is evidence of additional insured status
- [x] No — a certificate confers no coverage; an endorsement was required
- [ ] Yes, but only for the limits shown on the certificate
- [ ] Only if the certificate says "additional insured" in the description box

A certificate is a **snapshot of coverage that existed on the day it was
issued**. It creates nothing. Additional insured status comes from an
endorsement to the policy and nowhere else.

### A neighbour borrows the insured's car with permission and causes an accident. Whose policy responds first?

- [x] The car owner's — a permissive user is an insured under the owner's policy
- [ ] The neighbour's, because the driver is at fault
- [ ] Both equally, on a pro rata basis
- [ ] Neither; permissive users are excluded

Auto liability **follows the car**. The borrower's own policy typically sits
excess over the owner's. Lending the car lends the insurance with it.

### Which right does an additional insured normally NOT have?

- [ ] Coverage for liability arising out of the named insured's operations
- [x] The right to cancel or amend the policy
- [ ] Defence for a claim within the scope of the endorsement
- [ ] Coverage within the named insured's limit of liability

An additional insured **does not own the policy**. They gain coverage of a
defined scope and nothing more — no right to cancel, to change it, or, in most
forms, even to receive notice of cancellation unless the endorsement says so.

### A 19-year-old full-time student lives at university but keeps the family home as a permanent residence. Is the student an insured under the parents' homeowners policy?

- [x] Yes — a student away at school usually remains a resident of the household
- [ ] No, because the student does not physically reside there
- [ ] Only until age 18
- [ ] Only if the student is named on the declarations

Homeowners forms reach **resident relatives**, and **other residents under 21
in the care of an insured**. A student away at school who has not established a
separate permanent residence is normally still a resident of the household.

### Which party is merely notified of policy changes and has no right to any payment?

- [ ] Loss payee
- [ ] Mortgagee
- [x] Additional interest
- [ ] Additional insured

An **additional interest** (or interested party) gets notice only. A **loss
payee** has payment rights for specific property. A **mortgagee** under a
standard clause has payment rights plus protection from the insured's acts.

### A finance company is named for damage to a vehicle it financed. What is it called and what does it hold?

- [x] A loss payee, with payment rights in that specific property
- [ ] An additional insured, with liability coverage for the vehicle
- [ ] A first named insured, with the right to cancel
- [ ] A certificate holder, with evidence of coverage only

A loss payee's interest is **in the property**, not in the insured's liability.
It receives payment for damage to the property it has an interest in, and it
lacks the broader protections of a standard mortgage clause.

### Which of these is a duty of the first named insured rather than a right?

- [ ] Receiving return premium
- [ ] Cancelling the policy
- [x] Submitting to premium audit where the policy is auditable
- [ ] Requesting changes to the policy

The first named insured's position is a **bundle of rights and duties**:
notices, cancellation, changes and return premium on one side; the premium
obligation and responsibility for audit on the other.
