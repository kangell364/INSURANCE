### All of the following are requirements of an insurable pure risk EXCEPT

- [ ] The premium must be affordable
- [ ] The chance of loss must be calculable
- [ ] The loss must be accidental
- [x] The risk must be catastrophic for the insurer

**Every requirement protects the pooling; this one destroys it.** A risk is
insurable when a single event *cannot* wipe out many exposures at once — which
is why flood and earthquake sit outside standard property forms, and why Texas
handles coastal windstorm through TWIA.

**Non-catastrophic** is the requirement. "Catastrophic" is its inversion, and
inversion is how this stem is almost always built: the wrong option will be a
requirement turned inside out — the loss must be *intentional*, the insured
must be *able to cause* it, the risk must be *catastrophic*.

**So you do not need the list memorised in order.** Ask what each option would
do to the law of large numbers. Anything that breaks the pool is the answer to
an EXCEPT question.

### What does the law of large numbers allow an insurer to do?

- [ ] Guarantee that no individual insured suffers a loss
- [x] Predict aggregate losses accurately as the number of similar exposures grows
- [ ] Reduce the premium charged to each insured
- [ ] Eliminate the need for reinsurance

It makes the **group** predictable, not the individual. The insurer still
cannot say which house will burn — only roughly how many will, which is all
that pricing requires.

### Which is NOT a characteristic of an ideally insurable risk?

- [ ] The loss is definite in time, place and amount
- [ ] The loss is accidental from the insured's standpoint
- [x] The loss is certain to occur eventually
- [ ] There is a large number of similar exposure units

A loss **certain to occur** is not a risk but a schedule — that is why wear and
tear, rust and depreciation are excluded from every property form as
maintenance rather than accident.

### What is adverse selection?

- [ ] An insurer declining the worst risks in a class
- [x] The tendency of those most likely to suffer a loss to be the most eager to buy insurance
- [ ] A producer placing business with the insurer paying the highest commission
- [ ] A policyholder choosing a higher deductible than they can afford

It runs the wrong way for the insurer, which is why underwriting exists at
all — and why flood, sold voluntarily to a pool of people who expect to flood,
could not survive in a private market.

### Underwriting exists principally to do what?

- [ ] Maximise the number of policies sold
- [x] Select and classify risks so that each insured pays a premium matching their exposure
- [ ] Investigate claims after a loss
- [ ] Set the insurer's reserves

Selection and **classification** are the two halves. Refusing bad risks is only
part of it; sorting acceptable risks into classes that pay proportionate
premiums is the rest, and it is what makes the pool fair.

### What is the function of reinsurance?

- [x] It transfers part of an insurer's risk to another insurer
- [ ] It insures the policyholder against the insurer's insolvency
- [ ] It allows an insurer to avoid holding reserves
- [ ] It replaces the guaranty association

Reinsurance is **insurance for insurers** — it lets a company write a risk
larger than its own surplus would prudently allow. Protection against insurer
insolvency is the guaranty association, which is a different mechanism.

### In reinsurance, what is the ceding company?

- [x] The insurer transferring the risk
- [ ] The insurer accepting the risk
- [ ] The broker arranging the treaty
- [ ] The original policyholder

The **ceding** company cedes — gives away — part of the risk. The one accepting
it is the reinsurer.

### Which arrangement obliges the reinsurer to accept all risks of a defined class automatically?

- [x] Treaty reinsurance
- [ ] Facultative reinsurance
- [ ] Excess of loss on a single risk
- [ ] A reinsurance binder

A **treaty** is agreed in advance and applies automatically to the whole class.
**Facultative** reinsurance is negotiated risk by risk, with the reinsurer free
to decline each one.

### Premium is calculated from a rate. What is a rate?

- [x] The price per unit of exposure
- [ ] The total amount the policyholder pays
- [ ] The insurer's expected profit margin
- [ ] The percentage of losses paid to claimants

**Rate × exposure units = premium.** In workers' compensation the exposure unit
is each $100 of payroll; in property it is each $100 of insured value.

### Which of these best describes the pooling concept?

- [ ] Each insured pays for their own losses over time
- [x] The losses of the few are paid by the premiums of the many
- [ ] Insurers combine to share a single large risk
- [ ] Premiums are invested until a claim arises

That sentence is the whole mechanism. Insurers combining on one large risk is
**reinsurance** or a pool arrangement — related, but a different idea.

### An insurer's loss ratio is 78% and its expense ratio is 30%. What does this indicate?

- [ ] A profit on underwriting of 8%
- [x] An underwriting loss, since the combined ratio exceeds 100%
- [ ] That reserves are inadequate
- [ ] That rates are too high

78 + 30 = **108%**, so the insurer paid out $1.08 for every $1.00 of premium.
A combined ratio above 100 is an underwriting loss, which investment income
may or may not offset.
