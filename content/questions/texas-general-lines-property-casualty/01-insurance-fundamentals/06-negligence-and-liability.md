### What is a tort?

- [x] A civil wrong other than a breach of contract
- [ ] Any act that causes financial harm to another
- [ ] A criminal act for which damages may be recovered
- [ ] A breach of a duty created by a contract

**A tort is a civil wrong other than a breach of contract**, and it is the
category all liability insurance exists to respond to. Negligence is the
commonest kind; intentional torts and strict liability are the others.

**The exclusion of contract is the point.** If the duty came from an agreement,
breaking it is a breach of contract, not a tort — which is why liability
policies exclude liability assumed under contract unless it falls within an
**insured contract**.

A tort may also be a crime — an assault is both — but it is the *civil* wrong
that liability insurance concerns itself with. The state prosecutes the crime;
the claimant sues for the tort.

### Which is NOT one of the four elements a claimant must prove in negligence?

- [ ] A duty of care was owed
- [ ] The duty was breached
- [x] The defendant intended the harm
- [ ] Damages resulted

Negligence is **carelessness, not intent** — intent would make it an
**intentional tort**. The fourth element is proximate cause, linking the
breach to the damages.

**Do not conclude that liability policies never cover intentional torts.**
Harm the insured *meant to inflict* is excluded, but the **CGL's Coverage B**
covers a named list of offences — libel, slander, false arrest, wrongful
eviction — on purpose. See Module 4.

### A shopkeeper leaves a spill unmopped for an hour and a customer falls. Which element does the hour establish?

- [x] Breach of the duty of care
- [ ] Duty
- [ ] Proximate cause
- [ ] Damages

The **duty** exists because the customer is an invitee. The hour is what shows
the duty was **breached** — a reasonable shopkeeper would have cleaned it.

### What are compensatory damages?

- [x] Damages that restore the claimant's actual loss
- [ ] Damages awarded to punish the defendant
- [ ] Damages agreed in the contract in advance
- [ ] Damages limited to medical costs

Compensatory damages restore; **punitive** damages punish. The split inside
compensatory — special and general — is the next question.

### Which of these is an example of special damages?

- [x] $14,000 of medical bills and $6,000 of lost wages
- [ ] $50,000 for pain and suffering
- [ ] $200,000 in punitive damages
- [ ] $10,000 for loss of consortium

**Special damages are the ones with receipts** — measurable economic loss.
Pain and suffering and loss of consortium are **general** damages: real, but
not arithmetically provable.

### Why do most liability policies exclude punitive damages, or exclude them in some states?

- [ ] They are too expensive
- [x] Insuring a punishment would defeat its purpose, since the wrongdoer would not bear it
- [ ] They are rarely awarded
- [ ] They are not damages at all

It is a public-policy point, not an underwriting one. Some states permit
insuring them; others hold that doing so removes the deterrent entirely.

### A dog-walking business is liable when a walker's dog bites a passer-by. On what basis is the business liable?

- [x] Vicariously, for the employee's act within the scope of employment
- [ ] Absolutely, because dogs are inherently dangerous
- [ ] Under strict liability for animals
- [ ] It is not liable; only the walker is

**Vicarious liability attaches to somebody who did nothing wrong** — the
business was not careless, it is liable because of the relationship. That is
the examinable feature.

### What distinguishes strict liability from absolute liability?

- [x] Strict liability relieves the claimant of proving fault; absolute liability leaves the defendant with no defence at all
- [ ] They are identical terms
- [ ] Strict liability applies only to products; absolute only to animals
- [ ] Absolute liability requires proof of intent

Product liability is **strict**: no need to show carelessness, but misuse or
alteration can still be argued. Workers' compensation is **absolute**: the
employer pays regardless of fault and regardless of the employee's own
carelessness.

### Under comparative negligence, a claimant 30% at fault with $100,000 of damages recovers what?

- [ ] $100,000
- [x] $70,000
- [ ] $0
- [ ] $30,000

**Comparative negligence reduces the award by the claimant's share.** Under
pure *contributory* negligence — still the rule in a few states — any fault at
all would bar recovery entirely.

### What is an attractive nuisance?

- [x] A condition likely to draw children onto the property, raising the owner's duty of care
- [ ] A hazard the owner has been warned about but not fixed
- [ ] A trespasser's claim against a property owner
- [ ] A nuisance affecting neighbouring property values

Swimming pools, trampolines and construction sites. **The doctrine raises the
duty owed to a child trespasser** — a class who would otherwise be owed almost
nothing.

### Which defence argues that the claimant knowingly accepted a known danger?

- [ ] Comparative negligence
- [x] Assumption of risk
- [ ] Res ipsa loquitur
- [ ] Statute of limitations

**Assumption of risk.** *Res ipsa loquitur* is the opposite — it helps the
claimant, by letting negligence be inferred from an accident that ordinarily
does not happen without it.

### A jury awards $40,000 for medical bills and lost wages, and $60,000 for pain and suffering. How is each classified?

- [x] $40,000 special damages and $60,000 general damages — both compensatory
- [ ] $40,000 compensatory and $60,000 punitive
- [ ] $40,000 general and $60,000 special
- [ ] Both are general damages, since one award was made

**Sort by whether a receipt exists.** Special damages are specific, provable and
economic. General damages are real but not itemisable — pain and suffering, loss
of consortium, disfigurement. **Punitive damages are a third thing entirely**:
they punish rather than compensate, and are not awarded to restore a loss.
