### An insured's car is damaged in a collision they caused. Under their own collision coverage, this is what kind of claim?

- [x] A first-party claim
- [ ] A second-party claim
- [ ] A third-party claim
- [ ] A subrogated claim

**First party.** The insured is claiming against their own insurer for their
own loss. That the collision was their fault changes nothing — collision
coverage is first-party coverage regardless of blame.

The driver they hit claiming against that same policy's **liability** coverage
would be the **third-party** claim. "Second-party claim" is not a term; the
second party *is* the insurer.

This matters in Texas beyond vocabulary: the prompt-payment deadlines of
**§542.055–.058** run on first-party claims only.

### A liability policy is served with a lawsuit alleging something that later proves groundless. What must the insurer do?

- [x] Defend the suit, because the duty to defend is broader than the duty to pay
- [ ] Nothing, because a groundless allegation is not a covered loss
- [ ] Pay the claimant to end the suit
- [ ] Defend only after the allegation is proven

**The duty to defend is triggered by the allegation, not the outcome.** If a
suit alleges something the policy might cover, the insurer defends — and a
defence that ends with the insured owing nothing is the duty being performed,
not avoided.

That is why defence costs are usually paid **in addition to** the limit rather
than out of it.

### A fire closes a restaurant for four months. Which is the indirect loss?

- [ ] The fire damage to the kitchen
- [x] The income the restaurant did not earn while closed
- [ ] The cost of the fire department's attendance
- [ ] The smoke damage to the dining room

**Direct loss is physical damage; indirect loss is its financial consequence.**
The distinction matters because they are paid by different coverages — the
property form and business income respectively.

### An insurer pays a property loss in full and keeps the damaged goods. What are the goods called?

- [x] Salvage
- [ ] Subrogation
- [ ] Abandonment
- [ ] Residual value

**Salvage rights follow payment.** Note the contrast with the abandonment
condition, which stops the insured *dumping* damaged property on the insurer
and demanding the limit.

### A family leaves for a three-month trip, leaving the house furnished. How is the dwelling described?

- [x] Unoccupied
- [ ] Vacant
- [ ] Abandoned
- [ ] Untenanted

**Unoccupied means the people are gone; vacant means the contents are gone
too.** It is vacancy that suspends perils, so the distinction decides the
claim.

### After how long does vacancy typically suspend perils such as vandalism and theft?

- [ ] 30 consecutive days
- [x] 60 consecutive days
- [ ] 90 consecutive days
- [ ] Immediately on the property becoming vacant

**Sixty days** is the standard period. The suspended perils typically include
vandalism, glass breakage, theft, sprinkler leakage and water damage — and a
vacancy permit is how the insured buys them back.

### A wholesaler holds shifting stock across four depots. Which approach fits?

- [x] Blanket insurance, so one limit follows the stock wherever it sits
- [ ] Specific insurance at each location
- [ ] A reporting form with no coinsurance
- [ ] Scheduled coverage for each depot's maximum

Insured specifically, the stock is over-insured at one site and under-insured
at another on any given day. Blanket normally requires a **statement of values
and a high coinsurance percentage** in exchange.

### What does a binder do?

- [x] Provides temporary coverage on the same terms as the policy it anticipates
- [ ] Guarantees the insurer will issue the policy
- [ ] Confirms coverage to a third party
- [ ] Amends the policy in writing

A binder is **temporary coverage**. Confirming coverage to a third party is a
**certificate**; amending the policy is an **endorsement**.

### A contractor gives a property owner a certificate of insurance. What does the owner have?

- [ ] Additional insured status
- [ ] A binder
- [x] Evidence that a policy existed on the day the certificate was issued, and no coverage of their own
- [ ] Loss payee rights

**A certificate creates no coverage.** An owner who wants actual rights must be
added as an **additional insured** by endorsement — the single most common
misunderstanding in commercial lines.

### An endorsement conflicts with the printed policy form. Which governs?

- [x] The endorsement
- [ ] The printed form
- [ ] Whichever favours the insurer
- [ ] The declarations

The endorsement is the more **specific and more recent** statement of what the
parties agreed. Note that ambiguity between them would still be read against
the drafter, under the adhesion principle.

### A contractor's policy is rated on payroll. What is charged at inception?

- [x] A deposit premium based on estimated payroll
- [ ] The full premium, calculated from last year's payroll
- [ ] Nothing until the audit is complete
- [ ] A flat premium unaffected by payroll

Actual payroll cannot be known in advance, so a **deposit premium** is charged
and a **premium audit** trues it up at the end of the period.

### A contractor's payroll estimate was $400,000; the audit finds $700,000. What happens?

- [x] The insurer bills an additional premium for the difference
- [ ] The policy is cancelled for misrepresentation
- [ ] The extra exposure is uninsured
- [ ] Nothing, as the deposit premium is final

The extra payroll was **covered** all along — the insured was under-*charged*,
not under-insured, and the audit corrects it. Permitting the audit and keeping
records is a policy condition, not a courtesy.
