### An insurance agent fails to add a newly purchased building to a client's policy and it burns. Which coverage responds?

- [x] The agent's errors and omissions policy
- [ ] The client's commercial property policy
- [ ] The agent's commercial general liability policy
- [ ] The agent's employment practices liability policy

**E&O covers financial loss suffered by a client because of the insured's
negligent act, error or omission in performing professional services.** Failing to
place requested coverage is the classic producer claim.

### A company holds a Christmas party; a guest drinks too much and injures somebody driving home. Does the CGL respond?

- [x] Yes — the company is not in the alcohol business, so host liquor liability remains covered
- [ ] No, all liquor-related liability is excluded
- [ ] Only if the company holds a liquor licence
- [ ] Only up to the medical payments limit

**The CGL covers host liquor liability but excludes those in the business** of
manufacturing, distributing, selling, serving or furnishing alcohol. **Dram shop
laws** — making a server liable for injury caused by a patron served while
obviously intoxicated or under age — are why that exclusion matters.

### A corporation becomes insolvent and cannot indemnify its directors, who are personally sued. Which D&O insuring agreement responds?

- [x] Side A
- [ ] Side B
- [ ] Side C
- [ ] None; D&O requires a solvent company

**Side A pays the individual directors and officers where the company cannot or
does not indemnify them.** Side B reimburses the company for indemnifying them;
Side C covers the entity for securities claims. **Side A is the coverage a
director personally cares about**, because it survives the company's failure.

### An employee sues for wrongful termination. CGL, workers' compensation, or something else?

- [x] Employment practices liability
- [ ] Commercial general liability, under personal injury
- [ ] Workers' compensation Part Two
- [ ] Directors and officers, Side C

**The CGL excludes it and workers' compensation does not reach it** — these are
not bodily injuries at work, they are employment decisions. EPLI is claims-made
and usually carries a retention the employer bears on every claim, since small
claims are frequent.

### A surgeon retires. Claims may arise for years. What must be arranged?

- [x] A supplemental extended reporting period, or prior acts coverage on a new policy with the retroactive date preserved
- [ ] Nothing; occurrence coverage responds whenever the claim arrives
- [ ] An increase in the per-claim limit before retirement
- [ ] Conversion of the policy to an excess form

A **basic** tail is automatic and short; the **supplemental** tail is bought and
must be purchased within a short window after expiry. Tail coverage matters most
where a child was the patient, since the limitation period may not begin until
they reach majority.

### A hacker steals customer records and the customers sue. First-party or third-party cyber coverage?

- [x] Third-party — cyber liability, which pays what the insured owes others
- [ ] First-party, which pays the insured's own losses
- [ ] Neither; the CGL responds to privacy claims
- [ ] Both equally, on a pro rata basis

Cyber liability covers **privacy breach claims, regulatory defence and penalties
where insurable, network security liability and media liability**. Data
restoration and network business interruption sit on the first-party side.

### Against what standard is a professional judged?

- [x] What a reasonably prudent member of that profession would have done in the same circumstances
- [ ] The ordinary reasonable-person standard of general negligence
- [ ] Strict liability, regardless of care taken
- [ ] The contract's express terms only

This is **higher and more specific** than the ordinary negligence test, which is
why the profession's own practices are usually decisive.

### Why is professional liability usually written claims-made rather than occurrence?

- [x] Professional claims surface years after the work, and an insurer cannot price a promise whose claims arrive decades later
- [ ] Claims-made coverage is required by statute for licensed professionals
- [ ] Occurrence forms cannot cover economic loss
- [ ] Claims-made forms carry no retroactive date

The other two structural differences from the CGL: professional liability
**commonly covers economic loss**, not just bodily injury and property damage, and
**many forms require the insured's consent to settle**.

### Why do many medical malpractice forms require consent to settle?

- [x] Settlements are reported to national practitioner databanks and affect licensure
- [ ] State law forbids an insurer from settling a malpractice claim
- [ ] The insured must fund any settlement personally
- [ ] Consent is required only where the claim exceeds the limit

A settlement implies professional failure. The parallel provision on such forms is
the **hammer clause**, capping the insurer at what a refused settlement would have
cost.

### An employee is deceived by a fraudulent email into wiring $200,000 to a criminal. Where does that coverage sit?

- [x] On the first-party side, often sub-limited, as social engineering or funds transfer fraud
- [ ] On the third-party side, as network security liability
- [ ] Under media liability
- [ ] Under the CGL's personal and advertising injury coverage

It is sub-limited because **the exposure is large and the control is human rather
than technical**.

### Why is cyber coverage bought separately rather than relied on under a property or liability form?

- [x] Standard forms respond to physical damage to tangible property, and data is generally not tangible property
- [ ] Cyber losses are excluded by federal law from standard forms
- [ ] Cyber claims always exceed standard policy limits
- [ ] Standard forms cover cyber but only on a claims-made basis

Many forms now also carry **express cyber exclusions**, so the coverage is bought
deliberately or not at all.

### A claims-made professional policy is renewed with the retroactive date advanced by three years. What has happened?

- [x] Three years of prior acts are now uncovered, whenever a claim arrives
- [ ] The reporting window has been extended by three years
- [ ] Nothing; the retroactive date affects only the premium
- [ ] Coverage has broadened to include prior acts

The retroactive date **bars acts before it, whenever the claim arrives**. Watch for
a renewal that advances it — it is the quietest way to lose years of coverage.
