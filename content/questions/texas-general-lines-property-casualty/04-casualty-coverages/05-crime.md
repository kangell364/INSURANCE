### A thief enters through an unlocked window, steals a laptop and leaves no marks. Is this burglary?

- [x] No — burglary requires visible signs of forced entry or exit
- [ ] Yes, entering to steal is burglary regardless of force
- [ ] Yes, because the entry was unlawful
- [ ] Only if the premises were closed for business

**Two elements: forcible entry and visible marks. No marks, no burglary.** It is
theft, and may become a mysterious disappearance claim depending on the form.

### A courier carrying the day's takings is threatened with a knife and hands over the bag. Which crime, and which insuring agreement?

- [x] Robbery, under the outside the premises agreement — the courier is a messenger
- [ ] Burglary, under the inside the premises agreement
- [ ] Theft, under employee dishonesty
- [ ] Mysterious disappearance

**Robbery requires a person put in fear.** A **custodian** has care and custody of
property inside the premises; a **messenger** is one of those people outside it.

### A bookkeeper diverts $80,000 over four years. The scheme is found in year five, after the policy in force during the theft expired. Which form responds?

- [x] A discovery form, which covers losses discovered during the policy period whenever they occurred
- [ ] A loss-sustained form, since the losses occurred while it was in force
- [ ] Neither; the loss is uninsurable once the policy expires
- [ ] Both, sharing pro rata

**The discovery form is broader**, because employee dishonesty is typically found
long after it began. A **loss-sustained** form covers losses occurring during the
period and discovered within it or shortly after.

### An annual stocktake shows $30,000 of missing inventory and nothing else. Is that a covered employee dishonesty loss?

- [x] No — loss proved only by inventory computation is excluded
- [ ] Yes, an inventory shortage is direct evidence of theft
- [ ] Yes, up to half the limit
- [ ] Only if the shortage exceeds 10 percent of inventory

**A shortage on the books is not by itself proof of theft**, though such records
may support a claim otherwise proved.

### An employer discovers an employee stole $500 two years ago and keeps them on. The employee steals again. Covered?

- [x] No — coverage for that employee ended on discovery of the first dishonest act
- [ ] Yes, each act is a separate occurrence
- [ ] Yes, but only above the amount of the first theft
- [ ] Yes, if the employer reported the first theft to the insurer

**Discovery of a dishonest act ends coverage for that employee immediately**,
since the risk is no longer fortuitous.

### Which term is the broadest?

- [x] Theft
- [ ] Burglary
- [ ] Robbery
- [ ] Forgery

**Burglary breaks in, robbery threatens a person, theft covers both and more, and
mysterious disappearance is what is left when you cannot say which.**

### What must an employee intend for employee dishonesty coverage to respond?

- [x] To cause the employer a loss and obtain a financial benefit
- [ ] Merely to act carelessly with the employer's property
- [ ] To conceal a bookkeeping error
- [ ] To resign after the act

**Simple negligence, or a mistake, is not dishonesty.** The intent to cause loss
and gain financially is what makes the act covered.

### Four employees each steal under a $50,000 per-employee dishonesty limit. How does a per-loss basis differ?

- [x] Per loss applies the limit to each dishonest act or scheme regardless of how many employees were involved
- [ ] Per loss multiplies the limit by the number of employees
- [ ] Per loss applies only to acts discovered in the same policy period
- [ ] There is no practical difference

**Per employee** applies the limit separately to each; **per loss** applies it
once to the act or scheme. Where several employees collude, the difference is
large.

### An accountant is tricked by a fraudulent email into wiring $200,000 to a criminal. Which exclusion is the obstacle under a basic crime form?

- [x] Voluntary parting — the property was handed over
- [ ] Acts of the insured
- [ ] Accounting or arithmetical errors
- [ ] Trading losses

**Voluntary parting** — property handed over because the insured was tricked — is
why **social engineering fraud** became a separate insuring agreement rather than
sitting inside the standard form.

### What is a watchperson, in crime coverage terms?

- [x] Somebody retained specifically to guard the premises, whose presence some coverages require
- [ ] An employee with care and custody of property inside the premises
- [ ] An employee carrying property outside the premises
- [ ] An independent security contractor excluded from coverage

A **custodian** has care and custody inside; a **messenger** is outside; a
**watchperson** guards, and certain agreements condition coverage on one being on
duty.

### A pickpocket lifts a wallet from an employee's jacket without the employee noticing. Robbery?

- [x] No — robbery requires force or the threat of force against a person
- [ ] Yes, the property was taken from a person
- [ ] Yes, because the taking was unlawful
- [ ] Only if the employee later felt threatened

No threat, no robbery. It is theft, and the form the insured holds decides whether
anything is payable.

### What does forgery and alteration coverage reach?

- [x] Signing another's name without authority, or altering a financial instrument such as a cheque or draft
- [ ] Any counterfeit currency accepted by the insured
- [ ] Any fraudulent statement made to obtain property
- [ ] Unauthorised access to the insured's computer systems

**Money orders and counterfeit money** is a separate insuring agreement, as are
**computer fraud** and **funds transfer fraud**.
