### Under an HO-3, lightning damages the house and a mysterious unexplained cause damages the contents. Which loss is easier to establish?

- [x] The dwelling loss, because Coverage A is open peril
- [ ] The contents loss, because Coverage C has the broader peril list
- [ ] Both equally; the burden of proof is the same
- [ ] Neither is payable without a police report

**HO-3's asymmetry is the most-tested fact in this lesson: the dwelling is open
peril, the contents are named peril.** On Coverage A the insured need only show
a loss occurred and the insurer must prove an exclusion. On Coverage C the
insured must prove a listed peril — and "mysterious cause" will not do it.

### A tenant wants coverage for belongings and liability but not the building. Which form?

- [x] HO-4
- [ ] HO-6
- [ ] HO-2
- [ ] HO-8

**HO-4 is the renter's policy** — no Coverage A, because the tenant does not own
the building, but Coverage C, D, E and F all apply.

### Coverage A is $400,000. What are the usual limits for Coverage B and Coverage C?

- [x] $40,000 and $200,000
- [ ] $80,000 and $160,000
- [ ] $40,000 and $100,000
- [ ] $20,000 and $200,000

**B is typically 10% of A; C is typically 50% of A** (40% in some forms); **D is
typically 20–30%**. Set Coverage A and the rest follow, though each can be
increased for extra premium.

### A guest slips on the insured's steps and is injured. The insured is not negligent. Is anything payable?

- [x] Yes — Coverage F pays medical expenses regardless of fault
- [ ] No, liability coverage requires legal liability
- [ ] Yes, but only if the guest sues and wins
- [ ] Only the guest's lost wages, not medical costs

**Coverage F, Medical Payments to Others, is no-fault.** It pays an injured
guest's medical costs with no finding of negligence, typically within three
years of the accident.

### Does Coverage F pay for injuries to the insured's own resident family members?

- [x] No — it covers others, not the insured or regular household residents
- [ ] Yes, up to the Coverage F limit
- [ ] Yes, but only for injuries occurring off the premises
- [ ] Only for residents under 21

This is the point candidates miss. **Your own family's injuries are a health
insurance matter**, not a Coverage F matter.

### A homeowner's $12,000 diamond ring is stolen. The policy carries a $1,500 special limit on theft of jewellery. What is the position?

- [x] $1,500 is payable — it is a limit, not an exclusion — and the fix is to schedule the ring
- [ ] Nothing is payable; jewellery theft is excluded
- [ ] $12,000 is payable; special limits apply only to business property
- [ ] $1,500 is payable and nothing can be done to increase it

**Special limits are limits, not exclusions.** Scheduling the item by
endorsement — a scheduled personal property floater — raises the limit, broadens
the covered perils and commonly removes the deductible.

### Why does HO-8 exist?

- [x] For older homes whose replacement cost far exceeds market value
- [ ] For homes in high-crime areas
- [ ] For homes used partly for business
- [ ] For homes with more than four families

Insuring a large old house for full replacement cost in a low-value
neighbourhood would be unaffordable and would create a **moral hazard**. HO-8
settles on a **modified repair-cost basis** using common construction materials
and pairs that with a narrower named peril list.

### What is the difference between an HO-3 and an HO-5?

- [x] HO-5 covers contents on an open peril basis as well as the dwelling
- [ ] HO-5 adds earthquake and flood coverage
- [ ] HO-5 doubles the Coverage C percentage
- [ ] HO-5 removes all special limits on personal property

That is the whole difference. HO-3 is open peril on the dwelling and named peril
on contents; **HO-5 upgrades the contents to open peril** too.

### Why is Coverage A limited — often to a small default such as $5,000 — under an HO-6?

- [x] The association's master policy covers the building
- [ ] Condominium units are worth less than detached houses
- [ ] The unit owner has no insurable interest in the unit
- [ ] Coverage C is expected to absorb structural losses

The unit owner's Coverage A answers for **what is inside the walls** — fixtures,
cabinetry, improvements and betterments, and whatever the master policy leaves
to the unit owner. Read the master policy to know which.

### A fire makes the insured's home uninhabitable. The family's hotel and restaurant costs are $4,000 a month; they ordinarily spend $1,200 a month on the equivalent. What does Coverage D pay?

- [x] $2,800 a month, the increase in living expenses
- [ ] $4,000 a month, the total cost incurred
- [ ] $1,200 a month, the ordinary cost
- [ ] Nothing until the home is repaired

**Additional living expense is the increase**, not the total. The household would
have eaten and slept somewhere regardless; the policy pays the difference.

### A covered peril damages a neighbouring building and a civil authority prohibits access to the insured's undamaged home. Is anything payable?

- [x] Yes — civil authority loss of use, typically for up to two weeks
- [ ] No, the insured's own property was not damaged
- [ ] Yes, but only if the neighbour's insurer declines
- [ ] Only the fair rental value portion

Coverage D reaches a **civil authority prohibition arising from damage to
neighbouring property by a covered peril**, for a limited period.

### Windstorm blows down a tree on the insured's lawn. Is the tree covered under the trees, shrubs and plants additional coverage?

- [x] No — windstorm is not among that coverage's listed perils
- [ ] Yes, up to 5% of Coverage A
- [ ] Yes, up to the per-item cap
- [ ] Yes, but only the debris removal cost

The standard trap. The additional coverage carries **a percentage of Coverage A,
a per-item cap and a limited peril list** — and windstorm damage to a tree is not
on that list. Debris removal of a tree that falls on a covered structure is a
separate matter.

### On most homeowners forms, how is a Coverage C loss settled?

- [x] Actual cash value, unless replacement cost is added by endorsement
- [ ] Replacement cost, automatically
- [ ] Agreed value based on the scheduled amount
- [ ] Market value at the time of loss

**Contents are ACV in most forms.** Replacement cost on contents is a commonly
sold **endorsement**, not a default.

### Which Section II exclusion would most likely defeat a claim arising from the insured's part-time consulting work at home?

- [x] Business pursuits
- [ ] Motor vehicles
- [ ] Intentional injury
- [ ] Workers' compensation obligations

Section II excludes **business pursuits** and **professional services**. An
incidental business occupancy may be permitted by endorsement; a full business
needs a commercial policy.

### Which of these dwellings is eligible for a homeowners policy?

- [x] A four-family dwelling with the insured occupying one unit
- [ ] A working farm with a farmhouse
- [ ] A six-unit apartment building the insured occupies
- [ ] A vacant house held for resale

Homeowners eligibility runs to **up to four families with the insured occupying
one unit**, with a limit on roomers or boarders. **Farms are not eligible** — a
farmowners policy covers those.

### A fire destroys a home. Clearing the burnt wreckage from the site before rebuilding can start costs $6,000. Which part of the homeowners policy pays it?

- [ ] Coverage A, as part of the dwelling limit
- [x] Debris removal, one of the additional coverages included in the policy
- [ ] Coverage D, loss of use
- [ ] Nothing — site clearance is the owner's own cost

**Debris removal** is one of the small coverages a homeowners policy includes
without being separately bought. It pays to clear away what is left after a
covered loss.

It is worth knowing it exists, because the cost is real and beginners assume
the dwelling limit absorbs it. The amounts are capped, and the cap is usually
expressed as a percentage of the loss.

**Coverage D** is wrong for a reason worth holding: it pays the extra cost of
living somewhere else, not the cost of work on the house.

### A condominium association is hit by a loss its master policy does not fully cover, and bills every unit owner $4,000 towards the shortfall. Which coverage responds?

- [ ] Coverage A, dwelling
- [ ] Coverage C, personal property
- [x] Loss assessment
- [ ] None — an association assessment is not insurable

**Loss assessment** covers a unit owner's share when the association charges
all its owners for a loss the master policy did not meet.

It is included in a homeowners policy at a modest amount, and it matters
particularly on an **HO-6**, the condominium owner's form, where the owner is
exposed to exactly this. The other three options are all about the owner's own
property, and an assessment is not a loss to the owner's own property.
