# Three words, three meanings

In ordinary speech *risk*, *peril* and *hazard* are near-synonyms. You could
say "the risk of fire", "the peril of fire" or "a fire hazard" over coffee and
nobody would stop you.

The exam stops you. It treats them as three separate ideas, and a run of
questions turns on nothing harder than telling them apart. They are worth ten
minutes now because every later topic assumes them.

The one-line version:

- **Risk** is the uncertainty.
- **Peril** is the thing that causes the loss.
- **Hazard** is what makes the peril more likely, or worse when it happens.

## Risk

**Risk is uncertainty about loss.**

Notice what that does *not* say. It does not say the loss is likely, or large,
or imminent. It says the outcome is not known in advance. A building with
sprinklers, an alarm and a spotless history still carries risk, because nobody
can promise it will not burn.

The exam cares about one division above all others, because only one side of
it is insurable.

**Pure risk** offers two outcomes: a loss, or no loss. A house either burns or
it does not. A driver either has a collision or does not. There is no version
of the story where you come out ahead. **Pure risk is insurable.**

**Speculative risk** offers three: a loss, no loss, or a *gain*. Opening a
restaurant. Buying shares. Betting on a horse. **Speculative risk is not
insurable.**

> If a person could profit from the event, an insurer will not cover it.

That single sentence answers a surprising number of questions. When a stem
describes a situation and asks whether it is insurable, find out whether
anyone stands to gain. If they do, the answer is no.

The reason is not squeamishness about gambling. It is that insurance works by
pooling many similar exposures and predicting the total — which only holds if
losses happen *to* people rather than being chosen *by* them.

## Peril

**A peril is the cause of loss itself.**

Fire. Windstorm. Hail. Theft. Collision. Vandalism. **Flood. Earthquake.**
When a policy sets out what it covers, it is listing perils, and two
structures recur:

- **Named perils.** The policy lists the causes it covers. If a cause is not
  on the list, there is no coverage. The burden sits with the insured to show
  the loss came from a listed peril.
- **Open perils** (you will also see "all risk" or "special form"). Every
  cause is covered *except* those the policy excludes. The burden flips: the
  insurer must show the loss came from an excluded cause.

**A cause of loss does not stop being a peril because a policy excludes it.**
Flood and earthquake are perils. Standard homeowners and commercial property
forms exclude them, and that is a statement about the *policy*, not about what
they are — which is why you buy flood cover through the NFIP and earthquake by
endorsement or separate policy. The peril was always there; the question is
only who agreed to pay for it.

This catches people out, because flood is usually met as "the thing that is
hard to insure". If a stem asks what a flood is, the answer is **a peril**.
"Excluded cause" describes how a particular form treats it. "Catastrophic
risk" describes why insurers are wary of it. Neither is what it *is*.

That reversal of burden is the point, and it is tested. Open-perils coverage is
broader not because the list of covered causes is longer but because the
policy stops trying to list them.

A related idea travels with perils: **proximate cause** — the unbroken chain
of events from the peril to the damage. If a windstorm breaks a window and
rain then ruins the carpet, the proximate cause is the windstorm, even though
water did the visible damage. Coverage follows the proximate cause, not the
last thing that happened.

## Hazard

**A hazard is a condition that makes a peril more likely, or a loss worse.**

A hazard is not itself the cause. Oily rags in a basement do not damage
anything. They make fire — the peril — more likely, and worse when it comes.

The exam expects three categories, and the last two are where people lose
marks.

**Physical hazard** is a tangible condition of the property or its use. Oily
rags. An icy front step. Storing fireworks in a shop. Anything you could point
at.

**Moral hazard** is *dishonesty in the insured*. A person who has arranged a
convenient fire before, or who would inflate a claim, or who insures a
business that has stopped making money. The insurer's exposure rises because
of who this person is, not because of anything on the premises.

**Morale hazard** is *indifference caused by being insured*. Not dishonesty —
carelessness. Leaving a car unlocked because the policy will pay. Skipping the
roof repair because it is covered anyway. The person is not planning to cause
a loss; they have simply stopped trying hard to prevent one.

The distinction in one line: **moral is dishonesty, morale is carelessness.**

### Deciding between them

Recognising the three definitions is easy. Choosing between them under a stem
you have never seen is the part that costs marks, so use a procedure rather
than a feeling. Two questions, in order:

1. **Could you photograph it?** A condition of the property or its use —
   rags, ice, fireworks, a flat roof — is a **physical hazard**. Physical
   hazard is about the *thing*.
2. **If not, is the person dishonest or merely careless?** Dishonest is
   **moral**. Careless is **morale**. Both are about the *person*, and the
   only thing separating them is intent.

The reason this works is that the same facts can be any of the three. Watch
one situation move:

| The situation | Hazard | Why |
| ------------- | ------ | --- |
| A car is parked overnight on a street with frequent thefts | **Physical** | A condition you could point at. Nothing about the driver. |
| The driver stops locking it once comprehensive coverage starts | **Morale** | No intent to lose the car — just less care, and only since the policy |
| The driver leaves it unlocked hoping it is taken, to claim | **Moral** | Dishonesty. The exposure rises because of who this person is |

**Nothing in that table changed except what was going on in the driver's
head.** That is the whole distinction, and it is why the exam gives you the
person's motive somewhere in the stem. Find the motive and the answer follows.

A useful check on your reasoning: **moral hazard makes an insurer not want
this applicant at all.** Morale hazard makes them want a deductible. If your
answer would get somebody declined, it is moral.

Some texts add a fourth, **legal hazard** — conditions in the legal
environment, such as a jurisdiction whose courts award unusually large
verdicts, that increase the size of losses independently of anything the
insured does.

## Loss

Rounding out the set: a **loss** is the reduction, decrease or disappearance
of value. It is what actually happened, after the hazard made the peril more
likely and the peril occurred.

## How this is examined

Questions in this area are rarely difficult once the vocabulary is straight.
They are usually one of four shapes.

**"Which of the following is a hazard?"** — with perils among the distractors.
Ask yourself: is this thing the *cause* of a loss, or a *condition that makes
a cause more likely*? Fire is a peril. A frayed extension cord is a hazard.

**"Moral or morale?"** — the most common trap in the whole section. Look for
intent. Dishonesty, fraud, a motive to cause a loss → moral. Laziness,
indifference, "it's insured anyway" → morale.

**"Is this insurable?"** — apply the profit test. Any chance of gain makes it
speculative and uninsurable.

**Named versus open perils** — ask who has to prove what. Named: the insured
proves the cause is covered. Open: the insurer proves it is excluded.

## Check yourself

Before moving on, answer these without looking back.

1. A restaurant owner whose business is failing takes out a large fire policy.
   Which kind of hazard is that, and why?
2. A homeowner stops locking the back door because they now have a theft
   policy. Which kind of hazard, and why?
3. Is the possibility that a stock you bought will fall in value a pure risk
   or a speculative risk? Is it insurable?
4. Under an open-perils form, who has to prove what?

## Answers

Work through the four above before reading these.

1. **Moral hazard.** The motive is dishonesty: a failing business is worth
   more to its owner burnt than trading, so the insurer's exposure rises
   because of who this person is.
2. **Morale hazard.** No intent to cause a loss — only carelessness that
   appeared once the policy did.
3. **Speculative**, because a gain is possible. Therefore not insurable.
4. **The insurer** must prove the cause of loss falls under an exclusion. The
   insured only has to show a loss occurred.
