# Competitor teardown — what to capture, and what not to take

A capture sheet for buying a proven competitor's course (Kaplan, ExamFX,
A.D. Banker) and getting usable data out of it rather than impressions.

**Why do this at all.** The blueprint gives us complete topic coverage and says
nothing about **depth** — whether HO-3 needs "open peril dwelling, named peril
contents" or also the Coverage B percentage and the theft sub-limit on
silverware. Two courses can both cover 100% of the outline and differ hugely at
preparing somebody. A vendor with years of pass-rate feedback has calibrated
that depth empirically, and their course is the best available proxy for it.

That calibration is the thing worth buying. It is also the thing that is
perfectly legitimate to learn from.

---

## The line

**Structure, depth and pedagogy are ideas. Their expression is not.**

| Take freely | Never take |
| ----------- | ---------- |
| How deep they go on each topic | Their sentences, paraphrased or otherwise |
| How many questions, and per topic | Their questions, reworded or not |
| Question *formats* they use | Their distractors |
| What they drill hardest | Their explanations |
| How they structure a study plan | Their diagrams, tables, mnemonics |
| Which Texas items they emphasise | Any PDF, slide or export |

The reason is not squeamishness. A course built on a competitor's expression is
an infringement claim waiting to happen, and it is also **strictly worse than
what we can do**: they paraphrase the Insurance Code, and we work from the
statute. Copying them would mean copying a second-hand version of a source we
already hold in full.

The same rule as `content/README.md`: teach what the coverage does; never
reproduce somebody else's words.

---

## What to record, per topic

For each blueprint section (GK.I to GK.VI, TX.I, TX.II):

| Field | Why it matters |
| ----- | -------------- |
| **Page or minute count** | Their weighting versus the blueprint's. A section they spend 3× the outline weight on is one they know is hard. |
| **Depth: 1–5** | 1 = names the concept; 5 = worked numbers, edge cases, exceptions |
| **Specific facts they insist on** | The figures and rules they repeat. This is the calibration. |
| **What they skip** | As informative as what they cover — a topic in the outline they barely touch tells you it is thinly examined |
| **Question count** | Per topic, if visible |

## What to record, overall

- **Total question count** in the bank, and how many in a full mock
- **Mock length and time limit** — do they mirror 130 scored / 150 minutes?
- **Do they report a readiness figure?** On what basis? Do they claim a
  relationship to the state's cut score? (They cannot honestly — see
  `docs/exam-facts.md`.)
- **Question formats** — plain multiple choice only, or also "all of the
  following except", scenario stems, multi-step calculations?
- **How wrong answers are explained** — verdict only, or teaching?
- **What the study plan looks like** — linear, diagnostic-first, spaced?
- **Price, and what tier gets what** — the pricing in
  `docs/competitor-research.md` is third-hand and flagged low-confidence;
  buying one fixes that for at least one vendor.

## The three questions the teardown should answer

1. **Are our lessons deep enough?** Compare their depth score against ours,
   topic by topic. This is the main reason to do this.
2. **How many questions is enough?** We have no basis for a target beyond "a
   mock is 130". Their bank size is real evidence.
3. **What do they do about Texas?** We hold 30 chapters of the Insurance Code
   and cite sections in every Texas lesson. If they paraphrase without
   citation, that is our differentiator and it should be visible on the
   marketing page.

## Afterwards

Record findings **here**, in this file, as observations — "Kaplan spends 14
pages on coinsurance and works four numeric examples" — not as excerpts. An
observation is evidence. A paste is a liability.

Then the gap list becomes the content plan: every topic where their depth
exceeds ours is a lesson to extend, and every fact they insist on that our
lessons omit is a fact to check against the primary source **and add if it
holds up**.

Note that last step. Their insistence is evidence about the exam, not about the
law. Where a vendor's manual and the Insurance Code disagree, the Code wins and
the disagreement is worth recording — we have already found the blueprint
itself citing a repealed section, and a handbook typo in the exam code.
